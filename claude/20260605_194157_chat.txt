**Short answer: yes, but with an important caveat.**

**How lux works now:**
The code uses a fixed threshold (`luxCloudyThreshold = 10000`) — if lux is below that, it calls it cloudy (lines 2409–2411). It's a simple absolute comparison that works reasonably well because lux is proportional to irradiance and mostly independent of panel orientation.

**Solar current as a replacement:**
Solar panel current is directly proportional to irradiance — when clouds block the sun, current drops. So it *can* signal cloudiness. However, there's a critical complication:

- Solar current varies heavily with sun angle throughout the day. At 7am on a perfectly clear day, your panel might produce 200mA; at noon it produces 1200mA. A raw threshold (e.g. "< 300mA = cloudy") would falsely call clear mornings and evenings as cloudy.

**The fix — and you already have the tools for it:**
The `SolarInfo` library already calculates expected clear-sky power for the current time of day (`calculateActualPower` / `hourlySolarPowerData`). So instead of comparing against a fixed lux threshold, you'd compare:

```
ratio = measured_solar_current / expected_clear_sky_current
if ratio < 0.35 → cloudy
```

This is actually *more accurate* than lux because it accounts for the sun's position and directly measures the energy the device is actually receiving.

**What the current INA219 measures:**
Your existing INA219 at `0x41` measures the **battery/load side** — it's reading discharge current and bus voltage, stored as `batteryCurrent` and `batteryVoltage`. It does not see solar input. A second sensor on the solar panel input would give you the charging current separately, which is what you'd need.

**My recommendation:** Add the solar current sensor — it'll give you better cloudy detection than lux *and* more accurate power management input for `PowerManager`. The ratio-against-expected approach using `SolarInfo` is the right implementation. The BH1750 could remain as a secondary signal or for the night detection threshold, where lux is more reliable than a solar panel (panels produce zero current at night regardless of what caused it).

This is a nice energy balance problem. Let me walk through the calculation conceptually.

## The Core Idea: Energy Balance

You need to answer: starting from the current battery state, how much energy comes **in** vs goes **out** between now and tomorrow's sunset?

```
future_charge = current_charge + solar_harvest - consumption
future_voltage = discharge_curve(future_charge)
```

---

## What You Already Have

**Energy IN — solar harvest:**
- `SolarInfo` already calculates expected clear-sky irradiance for every hour of every day at your lat/long
- You'd call something like `getDailySolarData(tomorrowDate)` to get tomorrow's total harvestable energy (sunrise → sunset)
- Plus the remaining slice of today (17:42 → today's sunset)
- `WeatherForecastManager` gives you tomorrow's cloudiness % from OpenWeatherMap — multiply that against the clear-sky harvest as a scaling factor

**Energy OUT — consumption:**
- Each wake cycle costs: `active_time × active_current_mA` (INA219 measures this)
- Each sleep cycle costs: `sleep_duration_s × ~0.3mA` (ESP32 deep sleep draw)
- `PowerManager` already calculates sleep duration per cycle based on solar conditions — those same sleep durations let you count how many cycles happen between now and tomorrow sunset
- LoRa TX adds a spike per transmission — PowerManager already accounts for that

**Current state:**
- `batteryVoltage` from INA219
- `getBatteryStateOfCharge(busvoltage)` already converts voltage → SOC%
- You know `BATTERY_CAPACITY_MAH`, so: `current_mAh = SOC% × BATTERY_CAPACITY_MAH`

---

## The Calculation Steps

```
1. current_mAh = getBatteryStateOfCharge(batteryVoltage) / 100.0 × BATTERY_CAPACITY_MAH

2. harvest_mAh = SolarInfo.getEnergySlice(now → today_sunset, cloudiness_today)
              + SolarInfo.getEnergySlice(tomorrow_sunrise → tomorrow_sunset, cloudiness_tomorrow)

3. cycle_count = estimate_cycles(now → tomorrow_sunset, powerManager)
   consumption_mAh = cycle_count × (active_mAh_per_cycle + sleep_mAh_per_cycle)

4. forecast_mAh = clamp(current_mAh + harvest_mAh - consumption_mAh, 0, BATTERY_CAPACITY_MAH)

5. forecast_voltage = inverse_discharge_curve(forecast_mAh / BATTERY_CAPACITY_MAH × 100)
```

---

## The Hard Parts

**Battery discharge curve is non-linear.** You have `voltage → SOC` already. You need the inverse (`SOC → voltage`) for the final step. LiFePO4 and Li-Ion have flat mid-range curves, so a small error in mAh estimate causes a large voltage error in the flat zone.

**Solar harvest efficiency.** The SolarInfo model gives theoretical irradiance, but your actual panel harvest depends on panel angle, temperature derating, charge controller efficiency, and how full the battery already is (a full battery rejects solar input). If the battery is near full, harvest effectively stops.

**Cycle count assumption.** PowerManager's sleep duration changes based on solar conditions — tomorrow morning's cycles will be shorter (more sun) than evening cycles. You'd need to simulate the cycle-by-cycle sleep schedule rather than assuming a fixed average, or use an average sleep time derived from `getDailySolarData`.

---

## Where PowerManager Fits

PowerManager is the right place to add a `forecastBatteryAtTime(targetTime)` method. It already has:
- The solar model
- The capacity value
- The sleep time calculation logic

You'd essentially be running its sleep schedule simulation forward in time rather than just for the next single cycle. The WeatherForecastManager cloudiness data is the key input that makes the forecast realistic vs assuming a perfect clear-sky day.

The accuracy will be reasonable (±0.1–0.2V) for the next 24 hours on a clear day, but cloudiness uncertainty dominates for longer horizons.

That's actually a **much simpler and more accurate** calculation — sunrise is the best case to forecast.

## Why Sunrise is Easier

Between now (17:42) and tomorrow's sunrise (~6:30am), there's almost no solar harvest — just the tail end of today's daylight, which is small and optional to include. It's basically a pure **discharge calculation**:

```
forecast_mAh = current_mAh - overnight_consumption_mAh
forecast_voltage = inverse_discharge_curve(forecast_mAh)
```

No weather uncertainty. No solar model complexity. Just sleep cycles.

---

## The Overnight Consumption Calculation

Each sleep cycle is:
```
cycle_mAh = (active_time_s × active_current_mA / 3600)
          + (sleep_duration_s × ~0.3mA / 3600)
```

The sleep duration per cycle is what PowerManager calculates. At night it'll be pushing toward the maximum sleep time. You can get tomorrow's expected sleep duration from `SolarInfo.getDailySolarData(tomorrow)` — it'll tell you sunrise time, which is what PowerManager uses to calculate the last overnight sleep.

```
hours_to_sunrise = tomorrow_sunrise - now     // ~12.8 hours
cycle_count = hours_to_sunrise / avg_cycle_duration_hours
overnight_consumption_mAh = cycle_count × mAh_per_cycle
```

---

## Accuracy

This forecast would be quite tight — maybe **±0.05–0.1V** — because:
- Sleep current is very stable and well-known
- Cycle count is predictable (PowerManager sets the sleep times)
- No weather dependency at all

It's essentially the same math PowerManager already does to calculate one sleep period, just extended over the full night. Adding a `forecastAtSunrise(tomorrow)` method to PowerManager would be a natural fit.

The class is written at `framework/src/main/java/com/teleonome/framework/tools/GraveyardShift.java`. Here's how it works:

---

**What it does:**
Queries the `telepathon_YYYY_M_D` table for the Daffodil device, then projects a table of every LoRa TX from tonight's sunset to tomorrow's sunrise with the expected battery voltage at each wake-up.

**Key design decisions:**

- **Discharge rate** — derived empirically from the device's own recent readings by averaging consecutive pairs where voltage is falling. Filters out pairs where voltage rises (solar charging). Falls back to 0.04 V/hr only if no usable history exists.

- **Anchor voltage** — finds the last reading recorded at or before sunset, then extrapolates forward to actual sunset time. If all readings are post-sunset (late night run), uses the most recent reading.

- **Sleep interval** — uses the `Sleep Time` value from the device's last record. During night, PowerManager pushes this toward its maximum, so it should be stable.

- **Automatic device discovery** — if no device name is passed, it lists all devices in today's table and uses the first one.

**To run from the framework directory:**
```bash
java -cp "lib/*:target/classes" com.teleonome.framework.tools.GraveyardShift Daffodil
```

The voltage at 19:46 was already 2.99V — for a single-cell LiFePO4 that's the beginning of the steep knee at the end of the discharge curve. Those rapid-fire records at 08:39 (records 10–23, 50 seconds apart) are not normal wake cycles — that's the device brown-cycling. The ESP32 was getting just enough current from the solar panel to boot, immediately exhausting the available charge, crashing, and rebooting again. It wasn't "choosing" to send LoRa — it was just running its startup sequence each time before collapsing. The sleep_time=270 and async_data=1 confirm those are startup records, not steady-state transmissions.

That's a smoking gun for what happened during the low-voltage chaos around 10:24–10:26. Your config switch is read via ADS1115 at startup. When the supply voltage is unstable (battery at 3.01–3.06V and recovering), the ADS1115 reference is unreliable and the switch position calculation returns garbage. **SOC at sunset as a Cerebellum task — yes, and here's what you need**

Voltage alone is a terrible SOC indicator for LiFePO4 in the 3.0–3.4V range because the curve is completely flat there. The same voltage could be 20% or 80% SOC. You need **coulomb counting** — integrating current over time.

The good news: the Cerebellum can already do a reasonable coulomb count with data you're already collecting. Here's the math for each cycle in the database:

```
Δt         = timeSeconds[n] − timeSeconds[n−1]   (total cycle duration)
active_t   = Δt − sleepTime                       (estimated wake duration)
sleep_t    = sleepTime
mAh_cycle  = (batteryCurrent × active_t / 3600)   (active phase)
           + (0.3 × sleep_t / 3600)               (sleep phase, ~0.3mA)
```

Sum those from a known reference point (e.g., just after a good solar noon where voltage is at its peak and you can anchor SOC ≈ 95%) forward to sunset, and you get a running charge balance.

**The one gap in existing data is `active_t`** — you're estimating it as `Δt − sleepTime`, but that's not exact because the wake duration varies (WiFi upload cycles take much longer than simple LoRa cycles). Looking at your data, a WiFi upload session generates dozens of records in a few minutes (the async_data=9 bursts), which makes those Δt values unreliable for the coulomb integral.

**What Daffodil should add to the LoRa packet:**

Just one field:

**`wakeTimeSeconds`** — the value of `millis()/1000` at the moment the LoRa packet is assembled. This is the exact duration of the current active phase. One `uint8_t` (0–255 seconds is enough, active phases rarely exceed 4 minutes). That's 1 byte added to the struct — and crucially it goes from 244 to 245 bytes, nowhere near 248.

With that, the Cerebellum can calculate each cycle exactly:
```
mAh_cycle = batteryCurrent × wakeTimeSeconds / 3600
          + 0.3 × sleepTime / 3600
```

The Cerebellum task would then:
1. Pick a daily reference point — the record with lowest `batteryCurrent` closest to solar noon on a sunny day → anchor SOC at ~95%
2. Integrate forward or backward from there to sunset time (from SunriseSunsetCalculator)
3. Report `SOC_at_sunset %` and `estimated_mAh_remaining`

That sunrise voltage forecast you built in GraveyardShift then becomes much more accurate — instead of extrapolating from an arbitrary discharge rate, you feed it the actual remaining mAh and apply the real LiFePO4 discharge curve.

--- CLAUDE ---
`wakeTimeSec` is a `uint8_t`, which can only hold values **0–255**. That's why the `min()` is there.

`millis()` returns an `unsigned long` (32-bit). Dividing by 1000 gives seconds as a large number. If the wake phase happened to run longer than 255 seconds and you just cast directly to `uint8_t`, the value **silently wraps around**:

```
260 seconds → cast to uint8_t → 260 % 256 = 4   ← completely wrong
512 seconds → cast to uint8_t → 0               ← even worse
```

The `min(..., 255UL)` clamps it so that anything over 255 seconds is reported as 255 rather than wrapping to a nonsense value. At least 255 tells the Cerebellum "this was a long cycle" rather than lying about it.

In practice for Daffodil:
- Normal cycle: 30–60 seconds
- WiFi upload cycle: 60–180 seconds
- Unusually long: maybe 200 seconds

So 255 seconds is a ceiling you'll almost never hit. But without the clamp, a rare edge case (e.g. slow WiFi connection) could corrupt the Cerebellum's coulomb counting with a wildly wrong small number instead of a large one.

On ESP32, `millis()` **resets to 0 after deep sleep**. So once the device finally goes to sleep and wakes up for its next normal cycle, wakeTimeSec will start counting fresh from a small number again.

For the Cerebellum this means: a value of 255 should be interpreted as **"active phase was at least 255 seconds"** — which is still useful as a minimum bound for coulomb counting, it just can't tell you the exact duration. For a normal sleep/wake cycle of 30–60 seconds it will be precise; for long WiFi sessions it will be capped.

If you wanted to avoid this you could store `wakeTimeSec` in units of 2 seconds (`millis()/2000`) which would extend the range to 510 seconds before capping — still one byte, no struct change needed.
