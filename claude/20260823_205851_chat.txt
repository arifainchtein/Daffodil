Good news: pressure only depends on the *height* of the water column above the sensor, not the tank's volume or width — so you don't need a big tank at all, just height.

**Math**: 1 psi ≈ 0.70 m of water head, so your 0–5psi sensor reads full-scale at ~3.5 m. A short, wide bucket gives you almost nothing to test with:
- 5-gallon bucket (~30cm/12in deep) → only ~0.3 psi, ~6% of range — too small a signal to verify much.

**What I'd actually do**: get a few meters of clear vinyl tubing (cheap, any hardware store) and mount it vertically — up a stairwell, taped to a wall, hung from a ladder, whatever's nearby. Connect the sensor fitting at the bottom, run the tube straight up, and fill with water from a jug at the top with a funnel. This needs almost no water (just whatever fills the tube's inner volume) but gives you real, known head:

- ~0.7m of tube → 1 psi (20% of scale)
- ~1.75m → 2.5 psi (50% of scale, good midpoint check)
- ~3.5m → 5 psi (full scale, if you can find that much vertical clearance — a stairwell atrium works)

Mark the tube with a tape measure at a few heights, fill to each mark, and compare the psi your code reports (via `debug#1` serial output from `readTankPressure1()`/`readTankPressure2()`) against the expected value from height × 0.70. That validates both the sensor's linearity and the `(V-0.5)×1.25` conversion in the firmware without needing a real ag tank at all.

Same question as before — to restate simply:

- **0.5–4.5V** ("live zero"): 0V physically means "sensor unplugged / broken wire / dead," and 0.5V means "genuinely empty." Your code (or you, reading a multimeter) can tell those apart.

For a manufactured product (not just a one-off test), 0.5–4.5V is the clear choice — and there's now an additional reason specific to your ADS1115-at-5V design that I hadn't covered yet:

**ADC input margin.** The ADS1115 datasheet's absolute max input is VDD + 0.3V. With VDD = 5V, that's a hard ceiling around 5.3V. A "0–5V" sensor sitting right at 5.0V output leaves you almost no margin — any supply sag/ripple on the ADS1115's 5V rail, or a sensor slightly out of spec, or a manufacturing unit that reads 5.1–5.2V, starts eating into that 0.3V buffer. A 0.5–4.5V sensor tops out at 4.5V, giving you a full 0.8V of headroom below the ADS1115's ceiling — much safer across a production run where you can't hand-verify every board's rail voltage.

**Two more reasons that matter more at volume than for a single test:**
- **Live-zero fault detection** (already discussed): at scale, "sensor died/disconnected" silently reading as "tank empty" turns into real support tickets and false alarms in the field. 0.5–4.5V lets firmware flag near-0V as a fault instead of an empty tank.
- **It's the actual industrial standard.** True 0–5V output pressure transducers are less common and often can't swing all the way to the rails anyway (op-amp output stage limits), so their real-world min/max is murky and inconsistent between suppliers/batches. 0.5–4.5V is a defined, guaranteed spec you'll get consistently from any vendor, which matters when you're sourcing in quantity.

Stick with 0.5–4.5V. No firmware change needed — that's what's already implemented.
